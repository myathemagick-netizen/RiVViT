# Crystal Grid — the Russian Dodecahedral Layer

## What it is

In 1973 Nikolai Goncharov, Vyacheslav Morozov and Valery Makarov proposed that Earth carries a combined icosahedral–dodecahedral grid. Bill Becker and Bethe Hagens later extended it into the full UVG grid (62 points, 120 triangles). RiVViT adds the Russian layer only:

| | Icosahedron (existing) | + Dodecahedron (new) | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vortices | 12 | 20 | 32 |
| Riftlines | 30 | 30 | 60 |

The 20 new vortices sit at the centres of the 20 triangular faces between the existing riftlines. The 30 new riftlines join the centres of neighbouring faces, so **each new riftline crosses exactly one original riftline**.

## Two versions, shown as two layers

**Dodeca · Site-pinned (violet).** Face centres computed from RiVViT's own vortex coordinates: the three corners are summed as unit vectors and projected back to the surface. Because the vortices are pinned to real sites rather than a perfect solid, these centres are irregular. They are computed live, so they follow any change to a vortex.

**Russian Crystal · Ideal (coral, dashed).** The perfect solid in the standard Russian / Becker-Hagens orientation:

- vertices at both poles
- two belts of five at ±26.565° (arctan ½)
- rotated so the midpoint of the upper-belt edge between the Algerian and Indus vertices falls on Giza, 31.72°N 31.19°E

This reproduces the familiar grid latitudes of ±52.62° and ±10.81° for the dodecahedral points. Coordinates were constructed from that orientation rather than copied from a published table.

The topology is identical in both: the same 12 vertices connect by the same 30 edges. Only the positions differ.

## Tolerance

Bethe Hagens described grid points as areas rather than exact locations, noting important sites up to about 80 miles (~130 km) from the calculated point. Gaps at or under 130 km are marked ✓ below.
